Harbour lights return to Port Ellery as keepers relight the old beacon

By Marguerite Ashdown · 11 October 2026

PORT ELLERY - For the first time in thirty-one winters, the lamp at the top of the Skerrow Point lighthouse burned through the dusk on Friday, and a crowd of roughly four hundred people stood on the shingle below to watch the beam sweep across Marrow Bay. "It sounds daft, but I felt the whole town exhale," said Dorcas Venn, who kept the light with her late husband Harold until the Harbour Board switched it off in 1995.

The relighting is the work of the Skerrow Point Keepers' Trust, a volunteer group that began life as a Tuesday-night knitting circle in the back room of the Anchor & Plum. The Trust raised £412,000 over six years through bake sales, a sponsored rowing marathon and one memorably chaotic auction of donated driftwood sculptures. Its chairman, the retired ferry engineer Tobias Quillfeather, said the final cheque arrived in an envelope marked only "for the lamp, from a grateful sailor."

Restoring the beacon proved harder than raising the money. The original Fresnel lens, a four-ton drum of hand-ground glass prisms, had been crated and stored in a municipal warehouse in Dunmere, where a leaking roof left it streaked with mineral stains. Conservators from the Maritime Heritage Workshop spent fourteen months cleaning each prism with distilled water and cotton swabs. Two prisms were cracked beyond repair, and replacements were cast by a small glassworks in Teller's Cross using a recipe found in a Victorian ledger.

The lamp itself is thoroughly modern. Where the old keepers trimmed paraffin wicks by hand, the new light uses a sealed LED array that draws less power than a kettle, backed by a bank of batteries and a small wind turbine on the headland. Harbour Master Imelda Roake said the Trust had insisted on keeping the lens turning at its historic pace of one flash every nine seconds, now driven by a whisper-quiet electric motor in place of the original clockwork weights.

Not everyone welcomed the project at first. Fishermen on the Ellery quay grumbled that a decorative light would be of no use to working boats that already carry satellite navigation. Skipper Alasdair Pennywhistle, who has fished the bay for forty years, admitted he had been one of the sceptics. "I told them it was a waste of good money," he said, standing at the foot of the tower with his cap in his hands. "Then I came in through the fog on Saturday morning and saw that nine-second flash. I'll be buying the first round."

Meanwhile the Trust has rebuilt the keepers' cottage at the base of the tower as a small museum. Visitors will find the original logbooks, in which successive keepers recorded the weather, passing vessels and, on one occasion in 1962, "a seal of unusual size and poor manners loitering by the slipway." Schoolchildren from Port Ellery Primary have recorded audio tours of the exhibits, and the guestbook already holds more than six hundred signatures.

There is a practical benefit too. Coastguard officers say the revived beacon will serve as a daymark and a night reference for small craft entering the shallow approach channel, where three dinghies ran aground last summer. Sector commander Priya Halloran said the Coastguard had asked the Trust to register the light formally as a private aid to navigation, a step expected to be completed by the end of the month.

For Dorcas Venn, the evening ended quietly. After the crowd drifted back toward the harbour pubs, she climbed the 112 steps one last time with a flask of tea and sat beneath the lens as it turned. "Harold used to say a lighthouse is just a promise that somebody is paying attention," she said. The Trust plans to open the tower to visitors on the first Sunday of each month, with the lamp lit every evening from dusk until dawn.
